Hardware · UHF RFID · any bag you pack the same way twice

Glance. Then go.

A keychain fob clipped to a bag, box, bucket, case or truck bin. Pick it up and the fob checks that every tagged item is inside. Green means everything's there. Red means something's missing. No phone at the bag.

Working name: the gear tracker. A brand-name decision is pending (shortlist in build/20). Youth hockey is the first test case, not the product.

The problem

Anyone who packs the same roster of expensive items over and over finds out something is missing only after they have left.

One missing glove.

A $125 lacrosse glove left on a field or locker-room floor is gone within minutes. You find out in the driveway, not at the field. (The founders' own story — see About.)

One missing drill.

A $300 drill or multimeter left at a job site walks away within the hour. The truck pulls out with a tool bag that looks full.

One missing sample, lens or airway kit.

A jeweler's sample case, a photographer's lens roll, a medic's go-bag: the same list every time, checked by memory, until the one time memory fails.

AirTag finds the bag. This checks what's in it. Trackers follow the container; if an item is left behind, the bag still gets home and the tracker is satisfied. The only direct competitor (a pocket RFID scanner aimed at photographers, ~10 years shipping) needs a phone app at scan time. Paper checklists are the other alternative — usually skipped. Trades-tool trackers (brand-locked) were flagged by an independent review and are not yet researched.

How it works

Setup takes a few minutes. After that, it's silent.

STEP 1

Tag your gear — one tag per item

Passive UHF RFID tags, no batteries. One tag per item is a locked design rule: spread tags read 100%; tags bunched together read 29–43% (build/19).

STEP 2

Clip the fob to the bag

It lives there. Wakes on motion from deep sleep; no button, no app to open.

STEP 3

Pick up the bag. Glance.

The fob scans for a short window on pickup, compares against the list it stores, and holds the light until the bag stops moving. Green = go. Red = check.

Questions people ask

How is this different from AirTag?
AirTag tells you where your bag is. This tells you what's in it. Different problem, different product.
Do I need a phone to use it?
Only during setup, to say which tags belong to which bag. After that the fob runs on its own: pick up the bag, glance, walk.
Does it work when the bag is wet?
Wet gear is the hardest physics case for a fabric bag, and the one we tested first. A soaked, packed hockey bag read 12 of 12 tags on every pass in the July bench session (build/23). That is one bag in one house; a second-bag replication is on the plan.
Does it work in a metal truck box or a vehicle?
Untested. Metal shadows UHF and needs on-metal tags; a steel box is a harder case than a fabric bag. It is on the candidate-segment list with that exact question attached, not a claim.
How long does the battery last?
Not measured. The production design targets a month between charges; the number will be published when the current measurements on the Testing page exist. The current dev kit is a physics rig, not a battery-life demo.
How private is my data?
The item list lives on the fob and in the app on your phone. Nothing leaves your devices; v1 is local-only with no accounts. Tag IDs are opaque — a stranger with a reader can't learn what's in your bag.

The scan event

0 ms

Motion interrupt

Accelerometer wakes the MCU from deep sleep. A pickup-signature check keeps car rides from starting scans (planned; thresholds to be set from a drive capture).

boot

Reader rail up

MCU raises EN; reader boots and answers on UART. Boot time is UNKNOWN for the ODM module — bench test T4.

scan window

Multi-pass inventory

5 polling rounds, set-union of EPCs, compared to the stored baseline. Window length is unvalidated: the last tag arrived anywhere from <1 s to 38 s in the soaked bag (build/23), so "~30 s" is a target, not a spec.

verdict

Light holds until still

GREEN or RED on the light pipe, held until the bag stops moving (2 s debounce). Then EN low, rail off, back to sleep.

Testing · bench session 1 · interactive walkthrough

Bench session 1, step by step

One card per test from the plan (build/29 v2.1). Each card says which devices to use, how to set them up, where every wire goes, what can damage a sample, the exact command to run, and what to record. Press Go to start a run; press Done on each card as you finish it.

no run started
checking log…

When you press Go, the run is written to the page's shared log; the agent session reads that log while you test. If the shared log is not reachable (for example on the static copy), results stay in this browser and the chip says "saving locally only" — use Copy results as JSON to hand them over.

Read before touching anything (build/29 §3).
  • Antenna on before power, always. Press the IPEX straight down until it clicks; leave it on for the whole session (~30 mating cycles rated).
  • 5.0 V only. Never try the module at 4 V on a free sample. Direct laptop port or bench PSU — never a bus-powered hub.
  • Fit 470–1000 µF across module +5V/GND for T2–T5 (400 mA pulses through a thin harness brown out without it). Note it in results.
  • Back-powering: with +5 V removed, the adapter's TXD can feed the module through its input protection. For any "module off" test, meter the module +5V pad — if it reads > 0.3 V the test is invalid; unplug the adapter's TXD Dupont or fit a 1 kΩ series resistor.
  • Meter burden: a DMM on a mA range drops 0.4–2 V at 400 mA peaks → brown-out resets. Measure TX current with a bench PSU readout, an INA219, or the DMM's 10 A jack. mA/µA ranges only for idle and asleep.
  • Logic level: the adapter survives 5 V on its inputs; the M5StickS3 does not (3.6 V abs max). Meter the module's TXD idle (T0) before any ESP32 wiring.
  • ESD / thermal: bare M2320 by the edges, foil bag between sessions; one-minute cool-down between 30 s runs at 26 dBm.
